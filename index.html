What an AI Agent Is

QUICK TAKEAWAY

A prompt is a one-time request. An agent keeps a standing set of instructions, so it handles the same kind of task the same way every time. You will build an agent.

WHAT YOU NEED TO DO

  • Compare the prompt and the agent below.
  • Note the six parts an agent stores. You will write them in Step 06.

ONE-TIME

Prompt

  • One-time instruction
  • Useful for quick tasks
  • You repeat the context every time

Example: “Write an introduction for a report about stormwater runoff.”

REUSABLE

Agent

  • Reusable, task-specific assistant
  • Stores role, purpose, constraints, inputs, and output expectations
  • Better for repeatable workflows

Example: “CE 205 Technical Introduction Coach that asks for report context, drafts using the four-function framework, evaluates the draft, and suggests improvements.”

ANALOGY A prompt is like asking for help once. An agent is like training an assistant to help with the same kind of task repeatedly.

The Four Functions of an Effective Technical Introduction

QUICK TAKEAWAY

Readers ask What? Why me? How? An introduction answers through four functions: give background, reveal the gap, fill the gap by stating your purpose, and project the structure. Watch both videos: the first explains the framework, the second plans a real CE 205 introduction with it.

WHAT YOU NEED TO DO

  • Watch Video 1, then Video 2.
  • Name the four functions in your own words.
  • Try the find-all-four exercise below.
WHAT?What is the point of this document?The report’s subject and purpose.
WHY ME?Why does it matter to the reader?The reader’s need or reason for engaging.
HOW?How can the reader find the relevant information?The report’s scope and organization.
FUNCTION 1

Give Background

“Give background that orients the reader to the context.”

FUNCTION 2

Reveal the Gap

“Establish the need for the report by revealing a gap or problem worth addressing.”

FUNCTION 3

Fill the Gap: State Your Purpose

“Show how the report will fill that gap by stating the specific purpose.”

FUNCTION 4

Project the Structure

“Provide an idea or overview of the document’s organization.”

JOBS, NOT SENTENCE SLOTS The functions can overlap and come in any order, and one sentence can do two jobs. For WA01, the final sentence must still preview the report’s contents.

Source: Irish and Weiss, Engineering Communication: From Principles to Practice, 2nd ed., Chapter 3, Principle 6, Building Effective Introductions (pp. 100–108).

DEEP DIVE

DEEP DIVEThe four functions in depth

1 · GIVE BACKGROUND

  • Orient this reader: high-context readers need less, low-context readers more.
  • More is not better. Keep it relevant, accurate, and supported.
  • For WA01 peers, skip what every engineer knows and give the project-specific context.

2 · REVEAL THE GAP

  • The reason the report is needed: information the reader needs, or information the writer must obtain.
  • Never invent a deficiency. For a historical project, the gap can be what readers still need to understand.
  • Short reports may imply it.

3 · FILL THE GAP: STATE YOUR PURPOSE

  • Say clearly and specifically what the document does about the gap.
  • It can come early, even first.
  • Describing, investigating, explaining, and solving are different claims. Never claim work that was not done.

4 · PROJECT THE STRUCTURE

  • A roadmap: what the report covers, in what order, and its scope.
  • Short documents may imply it.
  • WA01 requires it in the final sentence.
DEEP DIVEOrder, audience, and genre

Choose the arrangement from the reader’s expectations and knowledge, the genre, the purpose, and the detail needed (pp. 104–105). Balance four tensions:

  • Purpose vs. context: state the purpose first, or build background first?
  • Brevity vs. significance: how much explanation before it is too long?
  • Credibility vs. focus: show understanding without overwhelming.
  • More vs. less context: what does this audience need?
DEEP DIVECan you find all four functions?

Find where each function happens in this practice introduction (fictional drainage scenario, Step 07).

To support that decision, this report identifies the information and evaluation criteria needed before an approach is chosen, first describing the site context, then the two candidate approaches, and finally the criteria for further evaluation.

Show where each function appears
  • Give background: “Selecting a drainage approach … depends on runoff and site conditions.”
  • Reveal the gap: “yet the project brief supplies neither measured runoff values nor site elevations.”
  • Fill the gap: state your purpose: “this report identifies the information and evaluation criteria needed …”
  • Project the structure: “first describing … then … and finally …”

Two sentences, four functions.

VIDEO 1 — GENERAL CONCEPTUnderstanding the Four Functions2:03

Transcript

Narration

Technical readers bring three questions to every document. What is the point of this document? Why does it matter to me? And how can I find the information I need?

An introduction answers those questions through four functions. The names come from our CE 205 textbook, Irish and Weiss, Principle 6.

Give background: orient the reader to the context.

Reveal the gap: establish why the report is needed, by showing a gap or a problem worth addressing.

Fill the gap, by stating your purpose: say specifically how this report will fill that gap.

And project the structure: give the reader an overview of how the document is organized.

The questions and the functions are not a one-to-one match. Background helps answer what, and why me. The purpose helps answer all three. The structure mainly answers how.

The functions are jobs, not sentence slots. One sentence can do two jobs. Here, the first sentence gives background and reveals the gap. The second states the purpose and projects the structure. Two sentences, four functions.

The functions also have no fixed order. When readers are new to the project, background usually leads. When they already know the situation, the purpose can come first.

Finally, keep the textbook and the assignment apart. The textbook gives general principles: in some short documents, the structure can be implied. Your assignment decides how to apply them. For WA01, the final sentence must preview the rest of the report.

Knowing the four functions is an important first step. But how do we decide what information belongs in each part of an introduction? In the next video, we'll use a planning method adapted from our CE 205 textbook to organize technical information before writing.

Narration

Here is how a writer moves from technical information to a planned introduction. The assignment is WA01, a report on a notable engineering project. Its introduction is one paragraph, and the final sentence previews the rest of the report. The practice topic is Dorton Arena, in Raleigh, North Carolina.

Start with the reader. WA01 is written for civil engineering peers. They know what tension and compression are, but they do not know this project. So they are high-context for engineering, and low-context for Dorton Arena.

Next, the purpose. The generic purpose is to explain. The specific purpose is narrower: explain how the cable-and-arch roof works, how the light roof stays stable, and why the project matters.

Now collect the technical information, and keep only what is verified. The roof spans approximately 300 feet without interior columns. Steel cables work in tension, and concrete parabolic arches work in compression. The arches bear on abutments, and they are tied together below ground.

Build the context. The situation is the challenge every large arena faces: a long span that keeps sightlines clear while carrying the roof safely. The background is this project's answer, cables and arches, and the motivation is a column-free interior.

Now the gap, and do not invent a failure the sources do not support. The real gap is what this reader still needs to understand: how such a light roof stays stable, and how its forces reach the ground.

Connect the purpose to the gap. Explaining how the system works together is exactly what fills that gap. That supports the main claim: Dorton Arena proved a permanent cable-supported roof could work at the scale of a major public venue.

Plan the scope and structure. Include the structural system, stability and foundations, and the arena's significance, in that order. Leave detailed calculations for the body.

Decide how the introduction will be organized. These readers need the project background first. And WA01 settles one choice for us: the purpose and the overview go together, in the final sentence.

STEP 05 OF 10LEARN THE FRAMEWORK

How CE 205 Structures an Introduction

QUICK TAKEAWAY

The four functions say what an introduction does. For WA01, the paragraph pattern says how: a topic sentence that makes a point, supporting sentences, and a closing sentence that previews the contents. The video rebuilds a weak opening step by step.

WHAT YOU NEED TO DO

  • Watch the walkthrough.
  • Study the one-paragraph pattern.
  • If you are writing WA01, check its requirements.

ONE PARAGRAPH

FIRST

Topic sentence

Makes the major point implicitly.

Introduces the engineering subject. Never announces the paper.

Often begins the background

MIDDLE

Supporting sentences

Develop the background, the gap, and the purpose.

Stay focused; detail belongs in the body.

Background · gap · purpose

LAST

Closing sentence

Previews the report’s contents, in order.

The one place the paragraph refers directly to the report.

Projects the structure (WA01)

WA01 introduction requirements

  • One paragraph.
  • A topic sentence that makes the major point without announcing the paper.
  • Logically ordered supporting sentences.
  • A final sentence that previews the report’s contents.
  • No first person, and no unnecessary references to the author or paper.

Other assignments may set different rules. Follow the one you are writing.

DEEP DIVE

DEEP DIVEWriting like an engineer
  • Technical accuracy. Support every claim. Never invent measurements, calculations, results, sources, or project conditions.
  • Professional language. Precise engineering terms; no jargon that does not help.
  • Complete sentences. Clear subjects and predicates; no fragments.
  • Organization. Informative topic sentences and a clear order of ideas.
  • Sources. Paraphrase and cite accurately. For WA01: no direct quotations, and ASCE citations.
